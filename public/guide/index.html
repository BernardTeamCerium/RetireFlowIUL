Start here: what an IUL is (and isn’t)

An Indexed Universal Life (IUL) policy is permanent life insurance with two parts working together:

  • A death benefit that pays your beneficiaries, generally income-tax-free.
  • A cash value account that can grow over time and that you can access while you’re living.

The “indexed” part describes how your cash value earns interest. Instead of being invested in the stock market, the insurance company credits interest based on how a market index (like the S&P 500®) performs over a set period, within limits. If the index goes up, you’re credited a portion of the gain. If it goes down, your credit is typically 0% instead of a loss.

The big idea: an IUL trades away some upside (through caps and participation rates) in exchange for protection from negative index years, while also giving you life insurance and tax advantages a brokerage account doesn’t have.
What it isn’t: an IUL is not a stock market investment, not a savings account, and not a get-rich-quick strategy. It works best as a long-term tool, funded consistently for 10+ years.

Question 1
What job do I want this policy to do?

Before you look at a single illustration, get clear on the main goal. The right design depends on it.

  • Supplemental retirement income: you want to build as much cash value as possible and take tax-advantaged income later. This usually calls for a policy “max-funded” with the lowest death benefit the tax rules allow.
  • Protection plus growth: you want a meaningful death benefit for your family now, with cash value as a bonus.
  • Legacy: you mostly want to leave a tax-efficient inheritance.

A policy designed for one of these jobs can look very different from one designed for another, even with the same premium.

Question 2
How exactly will my cash value be credited?

These are the dials that determine your interest credits. Ask your professional to show you each one.

Most policies let you split money across several index strategies. Also ask: Can the carrier change caps and participation rates? (Usually yes, within guaranteed minimums.) What has their renewal history been?

Index credits do not include dividends paid by the companies in the index. That’s one reason an IUL won’t simply match the market in strong years.

Question 3
What does it really cost?

IULs have real costs, and they matter most in the early years. Know these before you sign:

  • Cost of insurance (COI): the monthly charge for the death benefit. It rises as you age.
  • Premium loads: a percentage taken from each premium payment.
  • Administrative and per-unit charges: monthly policy fees, often higher in the first 10 years.
  • Rider charges: for optional benefits you add.
  • Surrender charges: if you cancel in the early years (often the first 10–15), part of your cash value is kept by the carrier.
Why it matters: charges come out of your cash value every month, even in a year when your index credit is 0%. A well-designed, well-funded policy keeps those costs small relative to the cash value over time.

Question 4
Is the policy designed for my goal, or for the biggest commission?

For a retirement-income goal, design is everything. A professional who specializes in this should be able to explain:

  • Minimum death benefit: keeping the death benefit as low as the tax code allows for your premium generally lowers cost of insurance and leaves more money working in cash value.
  • MEC limits: if you put in too much too fast, the policy becomes a Modified Endowment Contract (MEC), and loans and withdrawals lose their favorable tax treatment. A good design stays just under that line.
  • Death benefit option: many accumulation designs start with an increasing death benefit (Option B) during the funding years, then switch to level (Option A) before income starts.

Ask directly: “Is this designed for maximum cash value or maximum death benefit, and why is that right for me?”

Question 5
Can I fund it consistently?

The #1 reason IULs disappoint is underfunding. Universal life is flexible: you can often pay less than planned. But if the cash value can’t cover the monthly charges, the policy can lapse, and that can be expensive and trigger taxes if there’s an outstanding loan.

  • Choose a premium you’re confident you can keep paying for at least 10 years (or for however long the plan calls for).
  • Build your emergency fund and pay down high-interest debt first.
  • Ask what happens if you need to pause or reduce payments for a year or two.

Question 6
How will I actually take income in retirement?

This is where the “tax-free income” you may have heard about comes from, and where the details really count.

Withdrawals

You can generally withdraw up to your basis (the total premiums you’ve paid) without income tax. Withdrawals permanently reduce cash value and death benefit.

Policy loans

Beyond basis, most people take policy loans. The insurance company lends you money using your cash value as collateral. Loans from a non-MEC policy that stays in force are generally not taxable income. Two common loan types:

  • Fixed (standard) loans: the borrowed amount stops earning index credits, and you pay a set loan rate. More predictable.
  • Indexed (participating/variable) loans: the borrowed amount keeps earning index credits while you’re charged a loan rate. There’s more potential upside, and more risk if credits come in below the loan rate.
The trap to avoid: if loans plus interest grow too large relative to the cash value, the policy can lapse, and the outstanding loan gain can become taxable all at once. Ask whether the policy has an overloan protection rider and how income would be monitored and adjusted each year.

Question 7
What assumptions are behind the illustration?

Every IUL proposal includes an illustration: a year-by-year projection. It’s useful, but it is not a guarantee.

  • Look at the illustrated rate. Industry rules (Actuarial Guideline 49-A) limit how high it can be, but you can ask for a more conservative rate.
  • Ask for a stress test: the same design illustrated 1–2% lower. Does the plan still work?
  • Review the guaranteed column, which shows what happens at minimum credits and maximum charges.
  • Ask how often the policy will be reviewed (we recommend at least annually).

Where an IUL fits next to your 401(k) and Roth IRA

For most people, an IUL works alongside other retirement accounts, not instead of them. A common order of priorities:

  1. Emergency fund of 3–6 months of expenses.
  2. 401(k) up to the employer match. That’s an immediate return you don’t want to leave behind.
  3. Roth IRA if you’re eligible.
  4. Additional tax-advantaged savings, where an IUL can be a strong option, especially if you’ve maxed out other accounts, earn too much for a Roth, want downside protection, or also need life insurance.

Your checklist: 7 questions to ask any agent

Bring this list to your conversation. A good professional will welcome every question.

  • Is this policy designed for maximum cash value or maximum death benefit, and why is that right for my goal?
  • What are the current caps, participation rates, and floors, and what are the guaranteed minimums?
  • What are all the charges, and what is the surrender charge schedule?
  • How close is this design to the MEC limit, and how do we stay under it?
  • What happens if I need to reduce or skip premiums?
  • What type of policy loan would I use for income, and is there overloan protection?
  • Can you show me this illustration at a lower rate, and the guaranteed column?
